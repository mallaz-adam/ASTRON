## Brief 1 — Chapter 1, Unit 4: Galaxies and the universe

You are researching and writing one complete beginner astronomy unit for ASTRON,
a calm illustrated learning app with an old star-atlas style. Glaux is its wise,
warm owl guide. His occasional lines are short and clear; his presence never
replaces the explanation.

**Unit:** Chapter 1, Our place in space; Unit 4, Galaxies and the universe.
**Main question:** Is our galaxy everything there is?

Learners have studied Earth, the Sun and the Moon; our solar system; stars and
the Milky Way. Begin by recalling this briefly. Do not assume they know the
technical language of cosmology, redshift, dark matter or galaxy evolution.

Teach these outcomes:

1. Explain the relationship between a star, a solar system, the Milky Way,
   another galaxy and the universe.
2. Describe what a galaxy contains using simple language and useful pictures.
3. Distinguish an astronomical observation, an artist's reconstruction and a
   diagram, including what each can and cannot show.

Research first using NASA, ESA, major observatories, universities and reputable
textbooks. Cite exact pages supporting the claims. If browsing is unavailable,
say so and mark unverified claims; never invent a citation or measurement.

Write a connected lesson in clear English for a complete beginner. Give enough
explanation to follow the reasoning. Divide it into manageable Continue parts,
with a short introduction, explanations, visual exploration, guided practice
and recap. Suggest roughly 8–12 parts if appropriate; quality and a natural
sequence matter more than the count. Explain unfamiliar terms at first use.
Do not compress the whole lesson into isolated facts or one dense page.

Include: other galaxies beyond our own; one carefully sourced example; a gentle
introduction to different appearances; and the meaning of the universe. Treat
any distinction between the observable universe and the whole universe
carefully, without a misleading physical-edge drawing. Leave detailed distances,
light-travel calculations, expansion and the Big Bang for later units/chapters.

Provide at least these visual specifications:

- A nested relationship map, explicitly schematic and not to scale.
- An observed galaxy beside a labelled explanatory diagram, with a clear
  distinction between photograph/data and model.
- A simple comparison activity that checks categories or model interpretation.

For every proposed image give the original source page, exact credit/licence
requirements, an accessible description, and whether its colours are processed.
Do not assume an image can be reused merely because it appears on a NASA page.
For models, specify labels and controls plus the limits of the simplification.
No decorative image should carry an essential concept without explanation.

Provide two optional guided practice moments, with prompt, answer, hint and a
kind explanation after each attempt. They should test the taught ideas, not
names or numerical trivia. There is no compulsory unit exam: the ten-question
evaluation comes after all six units of the chapter.

Return: title and outcomes; complete learner-facing text divided into parts;
brief optional Glaux lines; visual and interaction notes; practice answer key;
recap; misconceptions addressed; glossary; claim-by-claim source table with
access date/confidence; and unresolved questions. Write in your own words.
Deliver a readable PDF and plain text I can copy. Do not implement code.

## Brief 2 — Greek story: The Pleiades

You are researching and writing an illustrated Greek sky story for ASTRON.
The aim is a beautiful, understandable narrative with character motives and
consequences, followed by a careful transition to the real sky. This is a story
experience in its own right, not a science lesson disguised as a myth.

**Story:** The Pleiades, the seven sisters.
**Prior experience:** The reader may have met Orion, but must not need to
remember his whole tale or a list of Greek names.

Research ancient sources and reliable classical scholarship before drafting.
Starting passages to inspect include Pseudo-Hyginus, Astronomica 2.21,
Pseudo-Apollodorus, Library 3.10.1, and Aratus, Phaenomena, the Pleiades passage.
Verify the passages yourself: they do not necessarily tell the same events.
State author/work/passage, translator and link. Separate Greek traditions from
later Roman-era retellings or witnesses. If a passage cannot be verified, flag it.

Starting source pages, not a substitute for research:

- https://www.theoi.com/Text/HyginusAstronomica2.html
- https://www.theoi.com/Text/Apollodorus3.html

First give a short comparison of the important versions. Recommend one coherent
version for the main story, explaining why. Do not silently combine different
endings, motives or identities. Mark invented dialogue, reconstructed scene
details and adaptation decisions outside the learner-facing text.

Draft an original, connected retelling divided into manageable illustrated
scenes. Introduce unfamiliar characters when needed. Prefer a small cast over
an opening list of names. Let the reader understand what happens, why it matters
and how one event leads to the next. Use plain, graceful language. Give the story
enough room to breathe; do not pad it to a word quota. Handle difficult themes
honestly and without graphic detail or romanticising unwanted pursuit.

Glaux may offer a short opening invitation, one useful observation and a closing
thought. Keep him sparing and warm. Suggest one or two optional details worth
exploring, such as another ancient version or an observation about the pattern.
Do not interrupt the story with compulsory quizzes or timed reading.

Finish by clearly leaving the myth and observing the real Pleiades. Research
the astronomy separately with NASA, ESA or an observatory. Explain the relevant
star-cluster idea simply. Do not promise that everyone sees exactly seven stars
or can see them from any location at any time. Give a scientifically checked
sky-identification concept; an actual chart needs location, date/time and
orientation. Label any schematic. Do not treat myth as evidence about the
physical origin of the stars.

For each scene suggest an artwork or illustration idea. Prefer verified,
reusable historical art, real sky observations and clear atlas diagrams. Provide
original image-source pages, artist/date when known, credit/licence requirements,
and accessible descriptions. Never invent an ancient artwork or assume reuse
permission from a search result.

Return: source/version comparison; recommended main version; complete retelling
by scene; optional details; Glaux lines; astronomy epilogue; image plan; source
and claim table with access dates/confidence; adaptation notes; and unresolved
questions. Write in your own words rather than copying translations. Deliver
a clean PDF and copyable plain text for review. Do not implement code.
