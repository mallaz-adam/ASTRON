# ASTRON: proposed learning chapters and Greek stories

Prepared 2 November 2026. Working curriculum, not an implemented course.
The owner supports a gradual beginner-to-advanced journey with complete chapters
and substantial units. Detailed manuscripts and application changes still follow
the research/review workflow below.
This expands the platform roadmap following the request for more chapters,
more than three units per chapter, black holes, cosmology and substantial Greek
stories. Scientific articles remain a later authoring phase.

Latest practice decision, 2 November: important exercises have dedicated phases.
An attempt and feedback allow Continue; a correct answer is not required.
This supersedes optional practice drawers for the shared illustrated reader.

## What this plan means

Seven proposed chapters contain 47 teaching units. Each chapter also has one
ten-question evaluation, outside that unit count. The number of units follows
the ideas needed; it is not a reason to pad a chapter. Units teach through short,
connected parts with Continue, definitions, useful pictures and guided practice.
There is no compulsory exam after each individual unit.

The first four units of Our place in space are implemented. Other entries here
are planned content, except existing material that can be reviewed and reused.
This document changes no content IDs, routes, locks, rewards or saved records.
The proposed placement of the existing stars material is recorded here; no
chapter has been moved in the application during this planning work.

## Progression and depth

The seven chapters are the first course architecture, not a limit on ASTRON's
future size. Keep the sequence understandable before expanding its scope.

| Learning stage | Chapters | How the learning develops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beginner foundations | 1–2 | Name and distinguish familiar objects, read pictures, explain everyday sky changes, and learn basic scale and light-travel ideas. |
| Explaining mechanisms | 3–5 | Follow causes and consequences, compare possible outcomes, use simple models, and introduce observations that support explanations. |
| Deeper investigation | 6–7 | Combine earlier ideas, interpret evidence, compare explanations, and distinguish a finding from an unresolved question. |

Advanced subject matter does not by itself create advanced understanding. A
black-hole unit can begin accessibly; later tasks should ask the learner to
reason from evidence and explain model limits. The initial course offers
conceptual depth, not a claim to replace university astronomy or mathematical
physics. Introduce any graph, ratio or equation gently before asking learners
to use it.

Possible later chapter families for discussion are **How astronomers know**
(measurement, instruments and uncertainty), **Gravity, space and time**
(a deeper conceptual treatment), and **Questions still open** (evaluating
competing explanations and research). These are future proposals, not extra
approved chapters or prerequisites added to the first course.

## A unit is a complete lesson

A unit should answer one substantial question. Its short parts are the reading
format; together they must form a connected explanation with a satisfying end.
Use enough parts for understanding and test their length with beginners rather
than enforcing a card count or filling a time quota.

Each research manuscript should contain:

1. **An invitation:** a concrete question, a useful picture and why it matters.
2. **A bridge:** briefly recall the earlier idea this unit needs.
3. **A connected explanation:** a clear sequence of causes, comparisons and
   examples. Define unfamiliar terms when they first appear.
4. **Visual reasoning:** pictures or a controllable model that explain the
   difficult idea, with captions and explicit limits. Decorative art can support
   atmosphere, but the lesson must also have explanatory visuals.
5. **Guided practice:** a prediction, comparison or evidence question with a
   useful explanation after an attempt. This remains ungraded practice.
6. **A misconception check:** address a likely misunderstanding through the
   teaching, rather than adding an unexplained warning at the end.
7. **A meaningful recap:** show what the learner can now explain and connect
   naturally to the next question. Preserve one main Continue action per part.

For example, the proposed unit **What a black hole actually is** should let a
learner explain the event-horizon idea using a labelled model, interpret what
an observer can receive, and distinguish a boundary from a material surface.
Its manuscript needs the gravity/light bridge first. Those outcomes require
several connected teaching parts, not merely a definition and a quiz.

## What makes a chapter complete

Before opening a chapter as a finished learning experience, require an approved
goal, the full unit sequence, clear prerequisites, reviewed manuscripts and
visuals, a recap connecting its ideas, and the ten-question chapter evaluation.
Plan assessment coverage alongside the goals; author the final questions after
the teaching is settled. Questions should test explanation, application and
picture/evidence interpretation as well as essential recall. Give specific
review guidance after an attempt.

Confirmed by the owner on 2 November 2026: chapters are separate collections
with their own units. After finishing a chapter's units, the learner completes
and submits its ten-question evaluation to unlock the next chapter. Evaluation
completion is the gate; no minimum score or forced successful retry is required.
Opening the evaluation alone does not complete it. Completed chapters remain
available for review. A future chapter without authored content stays marked as
being prepared rather than becoming a playable empty chapter. Optional retake
behaviour and detailed feedback still need design; this decision changes the
plan, not the current application's locks.

The path should show what is completed, available, prerequisite-locked or still
being prepared. Neither an unpublished manuscript nor a future chapter label
should be represented as a playable lesson. Greek stories remain a parallel
collection with their own narrative structure, not extra science units used
to increase a chapter's size.

## Proposed learning sequence

| Chapter | Main question | Units | Learner outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Our place in space | Where are we? | 6 | Distinguish worlds, stars, galaxies and the universe; reason about scale and light travel. |
| 2. Reading the night sky | Why does our sky change? | 6 | Connect the sky's appearance with Earth's motion, viewpoint and sunlight. |
| 3. How stars live and die | What makes a star shine, and what happens afterwards? | 8 | Explain stellar lives using mass, gravity and energy, with different possible endings. |
| 4. Black holes and extreme gravity | What is a black hole, and how could we detect it? | 7 | Explain the boundary, formation and evidence, and recognise what is still unknown. |
| 5. Galaxies in motion | How are stars gathered into larger structures? | 6 | Explain galaxy structure, interactions and the observations behind dark-matter questions. |
| 6. The universe's story | What does the Big Bang explain, and how do we know? | 8 | Connect expansion, a hot early universe and observations; separate evidence from open questions. |
| 7. Worlds beyond our Sun | How do we find other planets and investigate life? | 6 | Interpret simple planet-detection evidence without confusing possibility with discovery. |

This is a teaching recommendation, not an order prescribed by NASA or ESA.
Chapter 1 gives an overview; later chapters explain mechanisms rather than repeat
the overview. Each later chapter briefly recalls the ideas it needs. A short
question or cover can reveal an exciting destination before its full chapter is
available; any new preview mechanic needs a separate design decision.

### Chapter 1: Our place in space

1. Earth, the Sun and the Moon — implemented.
2. Our solar system — implemented.
3. Stars and the Milky Way — implemented.
4. Galaxies and the universe — implemented from the reviewed manuscript, 2 November.
5. Understanding sizes and distances.
6. Light takes time to travel.

Visual direction: move from labelled familiar objects to a nested map, then
scale comparisons and a learner-controlled light journey. Show where any
schematic stops being to scale. End-of-chapter evidence task: explain which
objects fit inside which larger structures and what distant light tells us.

### Chapter 2: Reading the night sky

1. Why the Sun and stars seem to move — Earth's daily rotation.
2. Why different seasons show different stars — our changing night-time view.
3. Why seasons happen — Earth's tilt and the angle/duration of sunlight.
4. Why the Moon changes shape — sunlight and viewpoint.
5. Eclipses and shadows — when the alignment is right.
6. Finding your way around a star map — direction, location and patterns.

Visual direction: turn Earth by hand; compare viewpoints; move the Moon around
an illuminated model. The sky map must state its location and viewing time.
End-of-chapter task: use a diagram to distinguish a lunar phase from an eclipse.
Safe observation guidance belongs in any real-world Sun activity.

### Chapter 3: How stars live and die

1. What a star is — revisit our Sun as a star, then introduce its workings.
2. Birth inside a cloud — gravity brings gas together.
3. What makes a star shine — fusion, explained as joining light atomic nuclei.
4. Why stars differ — mass, colour, temperature and lifetime; review existing lesson.
5. The long middle — gravity and internal pressure in balance.
6. Giants and the Sun's future — one route through stellar ageing.
7. When massive stars collapse — supernovae and different possible outcomes.
8. What remains — white dwarfs, neutron stars and an introduction to black holes.

Visual direction: a branching life map, heat/mass exploration and labelled
before/after observations. Do not imply every star explodes or becomes a black
hole. Retain the existing fuel-race model's explicit schematic label.
End-of-chapter task: justify different possible endings from the taught clues.
Scientific starting point: [NASA, Stars](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/).

### Chapter 4: Black holes and extreme gravity

1. Gravity, orbits and falling — a plain-language bridge from Chapter 3.
2. What a black hole actually is — introduce the event horizon as a boundary.
3. How a collapsing star can form one — not the fate of every star.
4. Outside and inside the boundary — what can reach a distant observer.
5. How we detect something dark — nearby motion, glowing gas and images.
6. Giants at galaxy centres — growth and the unsolved origin of early giants.
7. Colliding black holes and remaining questions — gravitational-wave evidence;
   limits of our account of the interior.

Visual direction: labelled light paths, an orbit comparison, a star-motion
evidence board and an observation-versus-simulation comparison. Avoid a universal
drain or tunnel picture. A model of the interior must be labelled theoretical.

Accuracy boundaries: a black hole is a region from which light cannot escape
to the outside once inside the event horizon. The horizon is not a solid
surface. Stellar collapse is a formation route; the origins of supermassive
black holes remain under investigation. Do not present a physical singularity,
a wormhole journey or Hawking radiation as something directly photographed.
Sources: [NASA, Black holes](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/) and
[NASA, Types](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/types/).

### Chapter 5: Galaxies in motion

1. Inside the Milky Way — our location and the evidence for its structure.
2. Different kinds of galaxies — compare labelled observations.
3. Gas, dust and new stars — why different regions look different.
4. When galaxies meet — gravitational interactions and changing shapes.
5. Unseen mass — why astronomers infer dark matter; what remains unknown.
6. Groups, clusters and the cosmic web — organising larger structures.

Visual direction: our inside view beside a labelled model; photographs with
scale/context; a simplified orbit/data comparison with its limits explained.
End-of-chapter task: identify what a picture establishes and what requires
additional measurements. Detailed claims and datasets need a dedicated research
pass before a manuscript is approved.

### Chapter 6: The universe's story

1. Cosmology: studying the universe as a whole — questions and observations.
2. An expanding universe — introduce changing distances and redshift gently.
3. The hot early universe — what the Big Bang model describes.
4. The first simple elements — hydrogen, helium and evidence from their amounts.
5. Ancient light across the sky — the cosmic microwave background.
6. From gas to the first stars and galaxies — building the later universe.
7. Dark matter and dark energy — different questions, substantial unknowns.
8. The earliest moments and possible futures — inflation as a proposed early
   episode; what observations do and do not establish.

Visual direction: a timeline that expands into separate readable eras; a
learner-controlled expansion model; a real CMB map beside a careful colour key.
Provide an evidence trail rather than only a spectacular beginning animation.

Accuracy boundaries: teach the Big Bang as the evolution of an early hot, dense,
expanding universe, not an explosion from a point into surrounding empty space.
Do not promise an established answer to what caused the universe or what came
before. Distinguish the well-supported hot Big Bang picture from unresolved
details of inflation. The CMB is an early light record, not a picture of the
first instant. Sources: [NASA, Cosmic history](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/overview/)
and [ESA, Cosmic microwave background](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Cosmic_Microwave_Background_CMB_radiation).

### Chapter 7: Worlds beyond our Sun

1. What an exoplanet is — planets around other stars.
2. A small dip in starlight — the transit method and its limitations.
3. A star's tiny motion — finding a planet through its gravitational influence.
4. Unexpected worlds — how planetary systems can differ from ours.
5. Atmospheres and possible habitability — clues, not a guarantee of life.
6. Searching for life responsibly — compare evidence, uncertainty and headlines.

Visual direction: a controllable transit with a matching light curve, a
star/planet motion model, then an evidence-comparison activity.
Sources: [NASA, Finding and characterising planets](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/how-we-find-and-characterize/)
and [NASA, Habitable zone](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/habitable-zone/).

## Greek stories: a parallel collection

Stories have their own narrative purpose and browseable home. They should not
be reduced to rewards or added to inflate the number of science units. The
recommended first collection contains eight sky stories including the existing
Orion. A ninth seasonal tale is a later option. These are editorial proposals,
not finished retellings or fully checked astronomical maps.

| Proposed story | Narrative focus / reason to read | Possible astronomy connection | Source anchor and review note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orion, the hunter | Preserve the existing introduction; review its chosen version | Recognising a sky pattern; star colours | Pseudo-Apollodorus, Library 1.4.3; Pseudo-Hyginus, Astronomica 2.34. Different deaths of Orion must not be silently combined. |
| The Pleiades, seven sisters | Family, pursuit and a place in the sky | Star clusters; apparent brightness | Pseudo-Hyginus, Astronomica 2.21; Library 3.10.1 for names. Distinguish seven mythic sisters from a promised count visible to every observer. |
| Andromeda, Perseus and Cassiopeia | A connected story of danger, choices and rescue | Neighbouring sky figures; distinguish Andromeda's constellation from its galaxy | Library 2.4.3; University of Exeter reproduces the passage. Do not automatically give Perseus Bellerophon's Pegasus episode. |
| Callisto and the bear | Transformation, separation and recognition | Ursa Major; northern-sky orientation | Library 3.8.2 records a version and alternatives. Check a separate ancient source before adding Arcas's familiar near-hunting scene or identifying a second constellation. |
| Orpheus and the lyre | Music, love and loss, told with restraint | Lyra as a pattern and its named stars | Pseudo-Hyginus, Astronomica 2.7, quoted on Theoi's Calliope page. The full Orpheus plot needs its own primary-text review. |
| Ariadne's crown | Loss, a new beginning and a crown among stars | Recognising Corona Borealis | Pseudo-Hyginus, Astronomica 2.5, quoted on Theoi's Ariadne page. Crown origins vary. |
| Pegasus and Bellerophon | A winged journey, achievement and limits | Recognising Pegasus | Library 2.3.2; Astronomica 2.18. Select and name the ending rather than merging accounts. |
| Castor and Polydeuces | Brotherhood and the wish to share a fate | Gemini; comparing its two named bright stars | Library 3.11.2; constellation placement requires checking the relevant catasterism passage. Introduce Polydeuces/Pollux as Greek/Latin name forms clearly. |

Optional ninth: Demeter and Persephone, sourced to the Homeric Hymn to Demeter.
Connect its cultural account of seasonal return with a separate modern
explanation of Earth's tilt; never treat the myth as physical evidence. Review
the actual text rather than assuming the popular half-year version.

## How a story should feel

Use a flowing illustrated tale divided into manageable scenes. Each scene has a
clear setting, character intention and consequence. Give unfamiliar names a
brief introduction. An optional detail should reveal something worth noticing,
not scatter unrelated trivia. Retain keyboard/touch access and readable text.

The ending can reveal the relevant real sky pattern with a clear transition
from myth to observation. Viewpoint, location and season matter; do not promise
every reader can see the same stars tonight. Glaux frames a question or offers
one useful clue; the narrative has room to breathe.

Every story research pack must identify its ancient version, major alternatives,
newly invented dialogue/scene details, visual sources, and any difficult themes.
Age-appropriate adaptation should stay honest about events and character agency.
Do not invent a myth to attach to black holes, the Big Bang or a modern discovery.

## Reusable research briefs

### One science unit

Research [chapter/unit title] for a complete beginner. State the prior knowledge,
three achievable learning goals and one main question. Use primary institutional
sources such as NASA, ESA, observatories, universities and textbooks. Give a
connected plain-language explanation divided into short learner-paced parts,
define technical words, and specify a useful picture or controllable model for
each difficult idea. Include guided practice, misconceptions, correct answers
with explanations, a recap, source links and claim confidence. Label model
limitations and scientific uncertainty. Provide PDF plus copyable text. This
is one unit; the ten-question evaluation belongs after the whole chapter.

### One Greek story

Research [story title] for ASTRON. Start with ancient passages and reliable
classical scholarship. Name the selected version, author/work/passage and
translation; list important variants separately. Draft a connected original
retelling in clear English with character introductions and manageable scenes.
Keep the tale rich enough to understand motives and consequences. Suggest
historically sourced artwork, one or two optional discoveries, and an accurate
transition to the real sky. Keep myth and astronomy explicit. Mark invented
dialogue and adaptation decisions, avoid copying translations, and flag unresolved
source questions. Provide PDF plus copyable text for review before coding.
